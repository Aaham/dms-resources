Cybersecurity and AI governance: a guide for leaders

This guide covers the cybersecurity and AI decisions that belong to leadership: who is accountable, what to protect first and which rules staff follow. The technical setup belongs with your IT staff or provider, so each part ends with questions to ask them.

Cybersecurity

9 steps

  • Make one person accountable

    Give a senior person responsibility for cybersecurity and have them report to leadership on a regular schedule. An outside IT provider can do the work, but your organization still answers for the outcome.

  • Keep a list of what you have

    List your systems, accounts and devices, and the outside companies that can reach them. Mark which ones hold sensitive information and which you couldn’t operate without, then protect those first.

  • Require multi-factor authentication

    Turn it on for email, banking, remote access and every administrator account. Start with email, because whoever controls a mailbox can reset the passwords on most other accounts. An authenticator app or a security key is harder to defeat than a code sent by text message.

  • Keep software up to date

    Ask who installs security updates on your computers, phones, website and network equipment, and how quickly urgent fixes go in. Include anything a vendor hosts for you. Attackers often get in through a flaw that already had a fix available.

  • Confirm payment changes by phone

    Before you pay a new payee or change someone’s bank details, call the requester at a number you already have on file, not one taken from the email or invoice. Criminals can imitate a familiar voice with AI, so place the call yourself and don’t rely on one you receive. Require a second approver above an amount you set.

  • Give people only the access they need

    Keep administrator accounts separate from the accounts people use for everyday work. Review who has access to what a few times a year, and remove access on the day someone leaves.

  • Back up your data and practice restoring it

    Keep a copy of critical data where ransomware on your network can’t reach it, such as an offline copy or a separately secured cloud backup. Restore from it at least once a year, so you know that it works and how long it takes.

  • Train staff and make reporting easy

    Show staff what phishing and payment fraud look like in their own jobs, and give them one place to report anything suspicious. Don’t penalize someone for reporting their own mistake, because you need to hear about it quickly.

  • Write a one-page incident plan

    Record who makes decisions, whom to call (your IT provider, cyber insurer, lawyer and bank) and how you would reach staff if email were down. Keep a printed copy. If money has gone to a criminal, call your bank straight away and report it at ic3.gov. A bank can sometimes stop a transfer if it hears quickly. Fill in the one-page incident plan at resources.strategydms.com/incident-plan.

Questions to ask your IT staff or provider

  • Which of our accounts still don’t have multi-factor authentication?
  • How soon after release do you install urgent security updates, and how would we know if one was missed?
  • When did you last restore our data from a backup, and how long did it take?
  • Who has administrator access to our systems, including your own staff and tools?
  • Which outside apps can read our email and files, and who approved them?
  • If you suspected a break-in tonight, what would you do in the first hour, and whom would you call?

AI governance

9 steps

  • Find out how AI is already used

    Assume staff already use AI tools, whether or not you have approved any. Ask each team which tools they use and for what, including personal accounts and the AI features built into software you already pay for. Make clear that nobody is in trouble for answering, or the list will be incomplete.

  • Publish a list of approved tools

    Say which tools staff may use, for which kinds of work, and where to ask about a tool that isn’t on the list. Use accounts the organization controls instead of personal ones, so you can manage access and remove it when someone leaves.

  • Set rules for what information goes in

    Sort your information into levels, for example public, internal, sensitive and regulated, and state which levels may be entered into which tools. Keep client, employee, financial and health information out of any tool you haven’t reviewed.

  • Read the vendor’s terms before staff sign up

    Check whether the vendor uses what you enter to train its models, how long it keeps your data, whether you can delete it and which other companies handle it. Free and personal plans often have different terms from business plans.

  • Keep a person responsible for every result

    Name who checks AI output before it is used, and give them the time and the authority to reject it. AI tools can state wrong information with confidence, so the reviewer needs to know the subject. A decision that significantly affects someone, such as hiring or eligibility for a service, should be made by a person, and the one affected should be able to ask for a review.

  • Limit what AI tools can reach

    An AI tool or agent connected to your email, files or other systems can read, and sometimes change, whatever that account can reach. Grant the least access the task needs, and review these connections the way you review staff access.

  • Start with one narrow use

    Choose one repeated task and measure how it performs today. Set an end date for the trial and decide in advance what result would make you stop.

  • Write the rules down and train staff on them

    A page or two is enough: the approved tools, the rules for information, who reviews output and where to report a problem. Train staff on it before they use the tools for work. Start from the AI rules for staff at resources.strategydms.com/ai-rules.

  • Review on a schedule

    Tools, vendor terms and laws change. Have leadership review new uses, incidents, exceptions and vendor changes at a set interval, such as every quarter.

Questions to ask an AI vendor

  • Will our prompts, files or outputs be used to train or improve any model?
  • How long do you keep our prompts, files, outputs and logs, and can we set a shorter period?
  • Which model providers and other subprocessors receive our information, and where is it stored?
  • How quickly will you notify us of a security incident?
  • Can we export our data in a usable form, and will you delete it when we leave?
  • How will you tell us about changes to the model, features, prices or terms?

Ask for each answer in the contract or the vendor’s written terms, not only from a salesperson. The AI vendor questions at resources.strategydms.com/vendor-questions have all 25, with space to record each answer.
